Early one morning, as the sun peeks over the trees, a person stands quietly at the edge of a park. They hold binoculars to their eyes and listen carefully. A robin is singing from a branch. A woodpecker taps somewhere in the distance. A flash of blue wings crosses the sky — a kingfisher! The person smiles and writes something in a little notebook. They are a birdwatcher, and they have just had a wonderful morning.

Birdwatching is one of the most popular hobbies in the world. People of all ages enjoy it, from young children to grandparents. You do not need to travel far or spend lots of money to begin. Birds are everywhere — in gardens, parks, schoolyards, and even busy city streets.

## What Is Birdwatching?

Birdwatching, sometimes called birding, is the hobby of watching wild birds in nature. Birdwatchers look for birds, listen to their songs, and learn to recognise different kinds. Some keep a list of every kind of bird they have seen, called a life list. Others simply enjoy being outside and watching birds go about their day.

There are thousands of different kinds of birds in the world, and each one is special. Some are bright and colourful, like parrots and kingfishers. Some are tiny, like hummingbirds, which can hover in the air like little helicopters. Some sing beautiful songs, while others make funny sounds — hoots, quacks, caws, and chirps.

Birdwatchers learn to spot birds by looking at their size, shape, colour, and the way they move. A robin has a bright red chest and often hops along the ground. A heron is tall and grey, and stands very still in shallow water waiting for fish. With practice, birdwatchers can recognise many birds just by their songs, even without seeing them at all.

## What Does a Birdwatcher Need?

One of the best things about birdwatching is that you can start with almost nothing. But a few simple tools make it much more fun. The most useful tool is a pair of binoculars. Binoculars make faraway birds look closer, so you can see their colours and markings clearly. A child-sized pair is light and easy to carry. Remember to hold them steady and move them slowly, so you do not scare the birds away.

A field guide is a book with pictures and descriptions of birds. When you spot a bird you do not know, you can look it up in the guide and find out what it is. Many birdwatchers also use a notebook to write down what they see — the date, the place, and what the bird was doing. Over time, the notebook becomes a wonderful record of your adventures.

## Where to Find Birds

Birds live in almost every habitat on Earth, so you can go birdwatching almost anywhere. Your own garden is a great place to start. Put up a bird feeder with seeds, and you may soon have regular visitors. A small dish of clean water also attracts birds, especially on hot days.

Parks and woodlands are wonderful for birdwatching. In the morning, when birds are most active, you can hear many different songs. Walk slowly along a path, stop often, and look up into the trees as well as down at the ground. Some birds feed in the treetops, while others scratch around in fallen leaves.

## Listening to Birdsong

Birdwatchers do not just use their eyes — they use their ears too. Birds sing for many reasons. Male birds sing to attract a mate and to tell other birds that a patch of trees or garden belongs to them. Parent birds use soft calls to talk to their chicks. Alarm calls warn the whole flock when danger is near.

Each kind of bird has its own song, and learning to tell them apart is like learning a new language. The robin sings a sweet, cheerful tune. The blackbird sings a rich, flute-like melody. The cuckoo calls its own name — "cu-koo!" — over and over again. Some birds, like the nightingale, sing mostly at night, filling the darkness with beautiful music.

## Being Kind to Birds

Birdwatchers love birds, and they always put the birds' safety first. First, keep your distance. If a bird flies away when you come close, you were too near. Use your binoculars to watch from far away instead, and never chase a bird or try to touch it.

Second, be quiet and gentle. Loud noises and sudden movements frighten birds, especially when they are feeding their chicks or sitting on a nest. If you find a nest, look from a distance and never touch the eggs or the baby birds.

Third, do not feed birds the wrong foods. Bread is not good for most wild birds, because it does not give them the food they need to stay healthy. Seeds, nuts, and fruit are much better. Finally, help birds by keeping their homes safe. Planting trees and flowers and leaving wild corners in a garden all help wild birds live happily.

## Why Birdwatching Is Wonderful

Birdwatching is more than a hobby. It helps scientists too. When birdwatchers write down what they see and share their notes, scientists can learn how bird numbers are changing and which birds need help. This is called citizen science — ordinary people helping real science just by watching birds.

Birdwatching is also good for you. It gets you outside in fresh air, it teaches you patience, and it helps you notice the small wonders of nature. And it is a hobby you can enjoy for your whole life. Every season brings different visitors. In spring, birds return from their winter homes. In autumn, new birds pass through on their way to warmer lands. No two days of birdwatching are ever the same.

## Fun Facts

- Some birds can fly thousands of kilometres on migration, travelling from one side of the world to the other and back again every year.
- The tiny hummingbird can flap its wings so fast that they become a blur, and it is the only bird that can fly backwards.
- Owls can turn their heads almost all the way around, which helps them look for food without moving their bodies.
- A group of crows is called a murder, a group of owls is called a parliament, and a group of flamingos is called a flamboyance.
- The lyrebird from Australia is a brilliant copycat — it can imitate the songs of other birds, and even sounds like camera clicks.
