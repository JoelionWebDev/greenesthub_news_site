Have you ever seen a police officer riding a horse down a busy street? It is a wonderful sight. The horse is tall and strong, the officer sits high in the saddle, and people often stop to look and smile. These special teams are called mounted police, and they have been helping to keep people safe for a very long time. A mounted police officer and their horse are a team. They train together, work together, and learn to trust each other completely. The horse is not just a ride. It is a partner, a friend, and a very clever animal that helps its rider every single day.

## What Do Mounted Police Do?

Mounted police officers do many of the same jobs as other police officers, but they do them from horseback. One of their most important jobs is patrolling. That means riding slowly through streets, parks, markets, and busy public places to watch over people and make sure everyone is safe. Because the officer sits so high up on a horse, they can see over crowds and over parked cars. They can spot someone who needs help from far away, and people in a crowd can spot them too. That helps everyone feel safer.

Mounted police are also very good at helping at big events. Think of a football match, a concert, a festival, or a parade. When thousands of people gather in one place, horses can move gently through the crowd and help guide people in the right direction. A horse is big and easy to see, so people naturally make space for it. Officers on horseback can also reach places that cars cannot, like narrow paths in a park or a muddy field after rain.

Sometimes mounted police help during searches. A horse can walk through woods, fields, and rough ground where a car would get stuck. The officer up high can look over tall grass and bushes. Horses can also help in emergencies, such as when roads are blocked or flooded and cars cannot pass. In all these jobs, the horse's height, strength, and calm nature make a real difference.

## Meet the Police Horse

Not every horse can be a police horse. Police horses need to be brave, calm, and friendly. They must stay steady when they hear loud noises, like car horns, sirens, drums, or cheering crowds. They must not be frightened by flags waving, balloons, or sudden movements. They also need to be strong enough to carry an officer and all their equipment for many hours.

Many police horses are large, sturdy breeds. Some are tall draught horses, the kind once used to pull heavy carts. Others are strong riding horses. What matters most is not the breed but the horse's character. A good police horse is gentle with people, patient, and willing to learn. Young horses that show promise go to a special training school, where they learn step by step.

Training takes time. At first, a young horse learns simple things, like walking calmly in a city street and getting used to traffic. Then it learns to stand still while crowds pass by, to walk past loudspeakers and flashing lights, and to stay calm when something surprising happens. Trainers use kindness and treats to reward the horse every time it does well. A horse that is treated with patience becomes a confident and happy partner.

## A Day in the Life of a Mounted Officer

A mounted police officer's day starts early, and the horse always comes first. Before the officer rides out, they check their horse carefully. They brush its coat until it shines, clean its hooves, and make sure its saddle and bridle fit comfortably. A happy horse is a healthy horse, so officers also check that their horse has fresh water and good food, like hay and oats.

Then the officer puts on their uniform, climbs into the saddle, and the pair set off for their patrol. A patrol can last several hours. The officer and horse may ride through city streets, around parks, and past schools and markets. Along the way, the officer talks to people, answers questions, and helps anyone who is lost or worried. Children especially love meeting police horses, and officers often stop to let them say hello and gently pat the horse's neck.

After the patrol, the work is not over. The horse is untacked, which means the saddle and bridle are taken off. The officer grooms the horse again, checks its legs and hooves, and gives it food and water. Then the horse can rest in its stable, which is like a cosy bedroom for a horse, with clean straw or shavings to lie on. Horses need plenty of rest after a long working day, just like people do.

## Fun Facts

- Police horses are trained to stay calm around loud noises, flashing lights, crowds, and even fireworks.
- A mounted officer can see much further than an officer on foot because they sit so high up.
- Police horses wear special shoes, a bit like trainers for horses, to protect their hooves on hard city streets.
- Many police horses love being patted and enjoy meeting children on patrol.
- When a police horse retires after many years of service, it usually goes to live a quiet, happy life in a field, where it is looked after and loved.

## Saying Hello to a Police Horse

If you ever meet a mounted police officer, remember to be polite and gentle. Always ask the officer before you touch the horse, and let the horse sniff your hand first. Move slowly, speak softly, and never shout or run up behind a horse. Horses are big and strong, but they are also gentle animals that like kind visitors.

Mounted police officers and their horses show us something wonderful: that people and animals can work together as a team. The horse gives the officer height, strength, and a calm, friendly presence. The officer gives the horse care, food, training, and love. Together, they keep our streets safe, help at big events, and bring smiles to everyone they meet. The next time you see a police horse, give it a wave. You are looking at one of the oldest and most special partnerships between people and animals in the world.
