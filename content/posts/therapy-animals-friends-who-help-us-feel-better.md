Have you ever felt sad, worried, or lonely, and then felt better just by stroking a soft, friendly animal? Many people feel calmer and happier when they spend time with animals. That is why some animals have a very special job. They are called therapy animals, and they visit people who need comfort, courage, and a smile. They go to hospitals, schools, libraries, and homes for older people, and everywhere they go, they bring joy.

## What is a therapy animal?

A therapy animal is an animal that has been trained to visit people and help them feel better. The most common therapy animals are dogs, because dogs are friendly, gentle, and love meeting new people. Cats can be therapy animals too, and so can rabbits, guinea pigs, and even horses.

Therapy animals are chosen because they are calm, patient, and happy to be touched. They must not be scared by strange places, loud noises, or wheelchairs, and they must enjoy meeting people they have never met before. A therapy dog will sit quietly while a child reads it a story, or rest its head on the lap of someone who is feeling sad. The animal does not need to do tricks. Just being there, warm and friendly, is the job.

## How therapy animals help people

When you stroke a friendly dog or cat, something wonderful happens inside your body. Your heart beats more slowly, your breathing becomes calm, and you start to feel relaxed. Spending time with animals can help people feel less worried and less sad. That is why therapy animals are so helpful for people who are ill, or who are feeling lonely.

In hospitals, therapy dogs visit children who are not feeling well. The children forget about their worries for a while as they stroke the dog's soft fur and talk to it. Some children who find it hard to read out loud in class find it much easier to read to a dog, because the dog never laughs and never says they are wrong. In homes for older people, a therapy animal visit is often the best part of the week, because stroking a friendly dog brings back happy memories and makes them smile.

## Horses that heal

Dogs and cats are not the only therapy animals. Horses help people too, in a special kind of therapy called horse therapy. Some children and adults who find it hard to walk, talk, or feel confident spend time with gentle horses. Riding a horse helps them balance and grow stronger, and looking after a horse teaches them to be calm and patient. Horses are big, warm, and gentle, and many people feel brave when they are near them.

Grooming a horse, brushing its mane, and feeding it carrots can be very calming. For children who find it hard to make friends, a horse can be a wonderful first friend, because a horse does not judge you. It just enjoys your company. Horse therapy takes place on farms and in riding centres, with trained helpers always close by to keep everyone safe.

## How do therapy animals learn?

Therapy animals do not just turn up and start work. They are carefully chosen and trained. A therapy dog must first learn all the good manners that any pet dog needs. It must come when called, walk nicely on a lead, and sit quietly. Then it is tested to see if it is calm enough for the job. The testers watch how the dog reacts to loud noises, sudden movements, and strangers.

The dog's owner is trained too. The owner learns how to visit hospitals and schools safely, how to keep the dog clean and healthy, and how to spot when the dog is tired and needs a rest. Therapy animals do not work all day. They visit for a short time, often just an hour or two, and then they go home to rest and play.

## A day in the life of a therapy dog

Imagine you are a therapy dog called Biscuit. Your day starts with a good walk and a tasty breakfast. Then your owner brushes your coat until it shines, because you must always be clean when you visit people. You put on a special bandana that shows everyone you are a therapy dog, and then you set off for the day's visit.

Today you are visiting a school. The children are waiting for you, smiling and waving. You walk in calmly, and the children take turns stroking you and reading you stories. One shy boy reads you a whole page, and his teacher smiles because he has never read so well before. After the school, you visit a hospital, where you sit beside a little girl who is feeling poorly. She strokes your ears and laughs for the first time that day. Then you go home, tired but happy, and curl up for a long sleep.

## The bond between people and therapy animals

Therapy animals remind us that animals and humans need each other. People have kept animals close for thousands of years, and animals have always given us comfort, help, and joy. Therapy animals are a beautiful part of that long friendship. They show us that kindness does not need words. Sometimes, all it takes is a wagging tail, a soft purr, or a warm nose to make a hard day feel better.

## How to be kind to therapy animals

If a therapy animal visits your school or your home, there are some simple ways to be kind to it. Always ask the owner before you stroke the animal, and stroke it gently, never pulling its ears or tail. Speak in a soft, calm voice, because loud shouting can frighten it. Let the animal come to you, rather than chasing it. And remember that the animal is working, even if its job looks like fun.

## Fun facts about therapy animals

- Therapy dogs visit hospitals, schools, libraries, and homes for older people.
- Some children find it easier to read out loud to a dog than to a person.
- Stroking a friendly animal can help your heart beat more slowly and help you feel calm.
- Horses help people too, in a special kind of therapy called horse therapy.
- Therapy animals are tested to make sure they stay calm around loud noises and strangers.
- Therapy animals only work for a short time each day, then go home to rest and play.
