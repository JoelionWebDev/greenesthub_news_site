High up in the mountains, where the rocks are steep and the wind is cold, lives an animal that can climb almost anything. It is called the ibex, a wild mountain goat with huge curved horns and feet that grip the rock like climbing shoes. If you ever see a photo of an ibex standing on a cliff so steep it looks like a wall, you will wonder how it does not fall off. The secret is in its feet, its balance and a whole lifetime of practice.

## The Goat of the High Mountains

Ibex live in the high mountains of Europe, Asia and Africa. The Alpine ibex lives in the Alps, the great mountain range that runs through countries like Switzerland, France and Italy. The Nubian ibex lives in the dry, rocky mountains of the Middle East and north-east Africa. There are a few kinds of ibex, but they all love the same thing: steep, rocky places high above the valleys.

Life up high is hard. The air is thin, the weather changes fast, and winter brings deep snow. But ibex are built for it. Their thick coats keep them warm, and their strong legs carry them up and down cliffs all day long. For an ibex, a steep rock face is not a danger — it is home.

## Horns That Grow Like a Crown

The first thing you notice about an ibex is its horns. Male ibex have very large horns that curve back over their heads like a giant pair of handles. The horns have ridges across them, a bit like the rings on a tree trunk, and they keep growing for the whole of the animal's life. Female ibex have horns too, but theirs are much smaller and slimmer.

The horns are not just for show. Male ibex use them when they compete with each other. During the breeding season, two males may stand on their back legs and clash their horns together with a loud crack. The one with the strongest horns and the most power usually wins. These contests look fierce, but they rarely cause serious injuries, because the horns are built to take the shock.

Horns also help scientists tell how old an ibex is, because a new ridge appears on the horn each year, a bit like counting rings on a tree.

## Feet Made for Climbing

The real magic of the ibex is in its feet. Ibex have cloven hooves, which means each hoof is split into two toes. The bottom of each hoof is soft and rubbery, which helps it grip the rock. The edges of the hooves are hard and sharp, so they can dig into tiny cracks and ledges. It is like wearing a pair of climbing shoes that never come off.

An ibex can stand on a ledge so narrow that you would think it was impossible. It can leap from rock to rock with ease, and it can climb up and down slopes that would make a human climber nervous. Baby ibex, called kids, start practising their climbing when they are only a few days old. They copy their mothers, hopping from rock to rock, and soon they are almost as sure-footed as the grown-ups.

This climbing skill keeps ibex safe. Animals that hunt them, like wolves and eagles, find it hard to follow an ibex up a cliff. When danger comes, the ibex simply climbs higher, to a place where its enemies cannot reach it.

## What Ibex Eat in the Mountains

Ibex are herbivores, which means they eat plants. In summer, they graze on grasses, flowers and herbs that grow between the rocks. They love the sweet alpine plants that grow in the mountain meadows. In winter, when snow covers the ground, food is harder to find. Then they eat dry grass, twigs, bark and even lichens, the crusty green-grey stuff that grows on rocks.

Ibex spend a lot of their day eating. They need plenty of food to keep their bodies warm in the cold mountain air. They also need to drink, and they find water in mountain streams and in pools left by melting snow. In dry places, ibex can go a long time without drinking, getting most of their water from the plants they eat.

## Life in a Herd

Female ibex and their young live together in herds. A herd is like a big family group, and it helps keep everyone safe. Many eyes are better than two when you are watching for danger. If one ibex spots an eagle circling above or a wolf below, it gives a warning whistle, and the whole herd knows to be careful.

Male ibex live in smaller groups of their own for most of the year. They only join the female herds during the breeding season in late autumn and winter. After that, they go back to their bachelor groups high up in the mountains.

In spring, the females give birth to one or two kids. The kids are born on a safe ledge, and within hours they are already trying to stand and walk. Their mothers look after them carefully, and the kids stay close to the herd as they grow.

## The Ibex That Came Back

The Alpine ibex has an amazing story. Long ago, people hunted it so much that it nearly disappeared altogether. By the 1800s, there were only a few Alpine ibex left, living in one small area of the Italian Alps. People thought the animal might be lost forever.

But then something wonderful happened. People decided to protect the ibex. A special park was created to keep them safe, and ibex from that last group were moved to other mountains across Europe. Slowly, the numbers grew. Today, there are many thousands of Alpine ibex living in the Alps again. It is one of the great success stories of animal conservation, and it shows what can happen when people work together to save a species.

## Why Ibex Amaze Us

Ibex remind us that animals can live in the toughest places on Earth and make it look easy. They climb cliffs that would scare a mountain climber, survive freezing winters, and carry a crown of horns that grows bigger every year. At Greennest Hub, we think the ibex is one of the bravest animals in the mountains. Next time you see a mountain in a picture, imagine an ibex standing on its highest, steepest rock, looking out over the valleys below like a king on a throne.
