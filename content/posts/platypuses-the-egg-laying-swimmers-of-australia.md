Imagine an animal with a duck's bill, a beaver's tail, webbed feet like an otter, and soft fur like a mole. It lays eggs, but it is a mammal. It swims underwater, but it has no gills. It sounds like a made-up creature from a storybook, but it is real. It is the platypus, one of the strangest and most wonderful animals in the world.

Platypuses live only in eastern Australia and on the island of Tasmania. They spend most of their time in rivers, streams, and lakes, swimming and hunting for food. A platypus is about the size of a small cat, with thick brown waterproof fur, webbed feet, and a flat tail that stores fat. When scientists first saw a platypus, they thought someone had stitched different animals together as a joke. But the platypus is very real, and very special.

## A Mammal That Lays Eggs

Most mammals give birth to live babies, but the platypus is different. It lays eggs, just like a bird or a reptile. The platypus is one of only a few mammals in the world that lay eggs. These egg-laying mammals are called monotremes. The other well-known monotreme is the echidna, which also lives in Australia.

A female platypus lays one to three small, soft eggs in a special burrow she digs in the bank of a river. She curls her body around the eggs to keep them warm, and after about ten days, the baby platypuses hatch. The babies are tiny, pink, and have no fur at first. They drink milk from their mother, just like other mammal babies do, but the mother platypus has no nipples. Instead, the milk comes out through her skin, and the babies lap it up from her fur.

Baby platypuses stay in the burrow for several months. Their mother leaves them to go and hunt, and then comes back to feed them. When they are about four months old, they are ready to leave the burrow and swim in the river for the first time.

## A Super Swimmer and Hunter

Platypuses are excellent swimmers. They use their front webbed feet to paddle through the water, and their back feet and tail to steer. When they dive, they close their eyes, ears, and nose, so they cannot see, hear, or smell underwater. So how do they find their food?

The platypus has a superpower. Its bill is covered with tiny sensors that can pick up the very small electric signals made by the muscles of other animals. When a small shrimp, worm, or insect moves in the water, the platypus can sense it with its bill, even with its eyes closed. This special sense is called electroreception, and it helps the platypus find food in muddy water where it cannot see.

Platypuses hunt for food on the bottom of rivers and streams. They dig in the mud with their bill, looking for insects, worms, shellfish, and small shrimps. They scoop up the food along with some gravel and mud, and store it in cheek pouches, like a hamster stores food in its cheeks. Then they come up to the surface and chew their food, using the gravel to help crush it.

A platypus can eat about a fifth of its own body weight in food every day. That would be like a child eating many, many meals every single day.

## Where Do Platypuses Live?

Platypuses live in rivers, streams, lakes, and ponds in eastern Australia and Tasmania. They like clean, fresh water with plenty of plants along the banks. They dig burrows in the riverbanks, where they rest and sleep during the day. A platypus burrow can be many metres long, with a special nesting chamber at the end where the female lays her eggs.

Platypuses are mostly active at dawn and dusk, when the light is soft and the water is calm. During the day, they usually sleep in their burrows. They are shy animals, and they do not like to be disturbed.

## Staying Safe

Platypuses have few natural enemies, but they still need to be careful. Foxes, eagles, and large fish can be a danger, especially to young platypuses. The platypus's best defence is to stay hidden and to be a very good swimmer.

Platypuses are not in danger of disappearing, but they need clean rivers and healthy riverbanks to survive. Pollution, drought, and damage to riverbanks can make life hard for platypuses. People in Australia work hard to protect rivers and keep them clean, so that platypuses and other animals can live there safely.

## Platypuses and People

When European scientists first saw a platypus, in the late 1700s, they could not believe it was real. They thought the animal had been made by sewing a duck's bill onto a mammal's body. It took many years before scientists agreed that the platypus was a real animal, and even longer before they understood that it laid eggs.

Today, the platypus is one of Australia's most famous animals. It appears on the Australian twenty-cent coin, and it is loved by children and adults all over the world.

## Fun Facts About Platypuses

- The platypus is one of only a few mammals that lay eggs.
- A platypus closes its eyes, ears, and nose when it dives underwater.
- Its bill can sense the tiny electric signals made by other animals.
- The plural of platypus is platypuses, not platypi.
- Baby platypuses are born without fur and drink milk that comes through their mother's skin.
- A platypus stores fat in its tail, like a camel stores fat in its hump.
- The platypus appears on the Australian twenty-cent coin.

The platypus is a true wonder of nature. It is part duck, part beaver, part otter, and all platypus. It lays eggs, swims like a fish, and hunts with a superpower hidden in its bill.
