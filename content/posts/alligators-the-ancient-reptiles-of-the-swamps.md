In the warm, still waters of the swamps, a pair of eyes rises slowly above the surface. Then a long snout appears, and a great, armoured body glides silently through the water. It is an alligator, one of the oldest animals on Earth. Alligators have lived in swamps and rivers for millions of years, since the time when giant reptiles roamed the land. They are true survivors, and they are perfectly made for life in the water.

Alligators live in the warm wetlands of the southeastern United States, and a smaller kind lives in China. They love slow rivers, marshes, lakes, and swamps, where the water is warm and the banks are muddy. They spend their days floating quietly, basking in the sun, and waiting patiently for food to come close. To a young explorer, an alligator looks like a living dinosaur, and in a way, it is.

## Built for the Water

An alligator's body is made for swimming. It has a long, powerful tail that pushes it through the water like a great oar. Its legs are short and strong, with webbed feet that help it paddle. When an alligator wants to move fast, it tucks its legs against its body and sweeps its tail from side to side, gliding through the water with surprising speed.

An alligator's eyes, ears, and nostrils sit on top of its head, so it can see, hear, and breathe while almost all of its body stays hidden underwater. Only a small part of its head shows above the surface, like a log floating in the water. This is how alligators sneak up on their food without being seen.

## The Powerful Jaws

Alligators are famous for their jaws. Their mouths are wide and flat, filled with rows of sharp, pointed teeth. When an alligator catches its food, it grabs it in a flash and holds on tight. Its jaws are very strong, and once an alligator has hold of something, it is very hard to get away. But alligators do not chew their food the way we do. They swallow their food whole, or in big pieces.

Alligators lose teeth all through their lives, and new teeth grow in to replace them. An alligator can grow many new teeth over its lifetime, so it always has a sharp set ready. Its teeth are made for grabbing and holding, not for chewing, and they are perfect for catching fish, turtles, and other animals in the water.

## What Alligators Eat

Alligators eat many different animals that live in and around the water. Young alligators eat insects, small fish, frogs, and snails. As they grow bigger, they start to eat larger fish, turtles, snakes, and birds. Big alligators can catch even larger animals that come to the water's edge to drink. Alligators are not fussy eaters, and they eat whatever they can catch.

Alligators help keep the swamp healthy. By eating fish and other animals, they keep the numbers of those animals in balance. They also dig holes in the mud called gator holes, which fill with water and give fish, frogs, and other animals a safe place to live during dry times.

## Basking in the Sun

Alligators are reptiles, which means they are cold-blooded. Their bodies do not make their own heat the way ours do. Instead, they warm up by basking in the sun. On a sunny day, you can see alligators lying on the muddy banks of a river or swamp, soaking up the warmth. They lie very still, with their mouths sometimes open wide, letting the sun heat their bodies.

When an alligator gets too hot, it slips back into the cool water to cool down. It moves between the sun and the water all day, keeping its body at just the right temperature. This is very important, because an alligator needs warmth to swim, hunt, and digest its food. Without the sun, an alligator would be slow and sleepy.

In the cooler months, alligators become quiet and still. They rest in the mud or in deep water, moving very little and eating almost nothing. They wait for the warm weather to return, when they will bask in the sun and swim again.

## Mothers and Baby Alligators

Mother alligators are wonderful parents. In the warm season, a mother alligator builds a big nest out of mud, plants, and sticks on the bank of a swamp or river. She lays her eggs inside the nest and covers them up to keep them warm and safe. Then she stays close by, guarding the nest from animals that might want to eat the eggs.

When the baby alligators are ready to hatch, they make high, squeaking sounds from inside their eggs. The mother hears them and helps to dig the nest open. The babies are tiny, with bright yellow stripes on their dark skin, and they look like little toy alligators. The mother gently carries them to the water in her mouth, holding them as softly as she can.

Baby alligators stay with their mother for a long time. She protects them from danger and teaches them how to find food and stay safe. The babies make chirping sounds to call their mother when they need help, and she comes quickly. As they grow bigger, their yellow stripes fade, and they become strong, independent alligators, ready to live on their own in the swamp.

## Ancient Survivors

Alligators are ancient animals. Their ancestors lived alongside the dinosaurs, and alligators have changed very little since then. They survived when many other animals did not, because they are tough, patient, and perfectly suited to their wetland homes. An alligator is like a living piece of history, swimming in the swamps of today.

Sadly, alligators were once in danger because people hunted them too much. But people worked hard to protect them, and now alligators are safe in many places. They live in protected wetlands, where they can bask, hunt, and raise their babies in peace.

If you ever visit a swamp in the warm south, look carefully at the water. You might see two eyes and a long snout gliding quietly through the reeds. That is an alligator, an ancient reptile of the swamps, patient and powerful, and perfectly at home in the water.
