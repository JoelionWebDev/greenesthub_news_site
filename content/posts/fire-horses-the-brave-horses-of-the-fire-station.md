Long ago, before fire engines had motors and sirens, the sound of a fire alarm meant something very different. It meant the thunder of hooves on the street, the jingle of harness bells, and a team of strong horses galloping out of the fire station, pulling a big red fire wagon behind them. Before fire engines had motors, brave horses pulled the fire wagons through city streets, learning every road and racing to save lives and homes.

## The Fastest Way to a Fire

In the days before cars, horses were the fastest way to get anywhere in a city. When a fire broke out, every second counted, because wooden houses and buildings could burn very quickly. Fire stations kept teams of strong, fast horses ready at all times, day and night, so they could rush to a fire as soon as the alarm rang.

The horses lived in the fire station itself, in stables right next to the fire wagons. Their harnesses hung from the ceiling above them, ready to drop down onto their backs in seconds. When the alarm bell rang, the horses would run to their places under the harness, and the firefighters would quickly buckle them up. Within moments, the team would be galloping out of the station, pulling the heavy fire wagon, the hoses and the water pumps behind them.

The horses had to be strong enough to pull a heavy load, and fast enough to get to the fire quickly. They were usually big, powerful horses, and they were trained to stay calm even when there was smoke, noise and shouting all around them. A good fire horse was worth its weight in gold, and firefighters loved their horses like members of the family.

## Horses That Knew the Way

Fire horses were so clever that they learned the streets of their city by heart. After a few trips, a horse knew the quickest way to every part of town, and it could find the way even in the dark or in thick smoke. Some horses were so well trained that they could find their way to a fire without the driver even holding the reins.

The horses also learned to recognise the sound of the fire alarm. When they heard it, they would get excited and stamp their feet, eager to run to their places. Some horses even learned to answer to the sound of their own station's bell, and they would ignore the bells from other stations. They were true professionals, and they took great pride in their work.

Fire horses worked in teams, usually in pairs or threes, and they learned to work together like a sports team. They pulled in step with each other, turned corners together, and stopped and started as one. The driver had to be skilled too, because steering a team of galloping horses through crowded streets was not easy. But the horses and the driver trusted each other, and together they made a wonderful team.

## The Dalmatians That Ran Alongside

Fire horses often had a special friend running alongside them: a Dalmatian dog. Dalmatians are the spotted dogs you may know from films, and long ago they were the companions of fire horses. The dogs would run in front of the horses, barking to clear the way through the crowded streets, and they would guard the horses and the fire wagon when the firefighters were busy fighting the fire.

The Dalmatian and the horse became a famous team, and even today, many fire stations keep a Dalmatian as a mascot, to remember the days when horses and dogs worked together to fight fires. The spotted dog and the brave horse are a part of firefighting history, and they are still loved by firefighters all over the world.

## Saying Goodbye to the Horses

In the early 1900s, motor fire engines began to appear, and they were faster and stronger than horses. One by one, fire stations began to replace their horses with motor engines, and the age of the fire horse came to an end. It was a sad day for many firefighters, because they loved their horses and did not want to say goodbye.

Some fire horses were so used to their work that they were confused when the motor engines arrived. There are stories of horses that ran to their places when the alarm rang, even after the motors had taken over, and of horses that tried to follow the fire engines out of the station. The horses had loved their jobs, and they did not want to stop.

Many retired fire horses went to live on farms, where they spent their days in green fields, pulling carts and ploughs. Some of them were given special retirement ceremonies, and firefighters thanked them for their years of brave service. Today, you can still see fire horses in old photographs, in museums, and in parades, where they remind us of a time when horses were the heroes of the fire station.

## Remembering the Brave Horses

Fire horses are remembered as heroes, because they helped save many lives and homes. They were brave, strong and loyal, and they worked hard every day, in all kinds of weather, without ever complaining. They were part of the family at the fire station, and the firefighters who worked with them never forgot them.

The story of the fire horses teaches us something important: that animals and people can work together to do amazing things. The horses did not understand fire, but they understood their job, and they did it with all their hearts. They are a wonderful example of the friendship between animals and humans, and of the brave work that animals have done for people throughout history.

Next time you hear a fire engine's siren, think of the fire horses, the brave horses of the fire station, galloping through the streets with bells jingling and hooves thundering, on their way to save the day.
