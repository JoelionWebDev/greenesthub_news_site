If you saw a gharial, you would probably stare. It is a kind of crocodile, but it looks very different from the ones you might know. It has a long, thin snout, like a pair of chopsticks, filled with many sharp, needle-like teeth. It lives in the great rivers of India and Nepal, and it is one of the best fish-catchers in the animal kingdom. The gharial is also one of the rarest crocodiles in the world, and people are working hard to save it.

## A Snout Like No Other

The gharial's snout is its most amazing feature. It is long and narrow, perfect for sweeping through the water to catch fish. When a fish swims close, the gharial snaps its jaws shut sideways, trapping the fish between its many teeth. It is a bit like catching a ball in a pair of tongs. The teeth are sharp and pointy, made for holding slippery fish, not for tearing big animals apart.

Male gharials have something extra: a strange, round, bulbous growth on the tip of their nose. It looks a bit like a small pot or a ball stuck on the end of the snout. This growth is called a ghara, which is the Hindi word for a pot. That is how the gharial got its name! The ghara helps the male make buzzing and humming sounds to call to females. It also makes him look impressive to other males.

## A Gentle Giant

Gharials are big animals. They are among the longest of all crocodiles, and an adult can be longer than a small car. But despite their size and their many teeth, gharials are not dangerous to people. They eat fish, and almost nothing else. Their thin snouts are too weak to catch large animals, and they are shy creatures that prefer to slide into the water and swim away when people come near.

Gharials spend much of their day basking in the sun on sandy riverbanks. Basking means lying still in the warm sunshine to heat up their bodies. Like all reptiles, gharials are cold-blooded, which means they need the sun's warmth to stay active. You will often see several gharials lying side by side on a sandbank, all facing the sun, looking like logs with legs.

When they are in the water, gharials are graceful swimmers. They use their powerful tails to push themselves through the river, and their legs tuck back against their bodies to make them more streamlined. On land, though, they are clumsy. Their legs are weak, and they can only slide along on their bellies. That is why they stay close to the water.

## Life in the Great Rivers

Gharials live in the rivers of the Indian subcontinent, especially the Ganges and its tributaries, and in rivers in Nepal. These are big, slow-moving rivers with sandy banks. The gharial needs deep water for fishing and sandy banks for basking and laying eggs. It is a creature perfectly suited to its river home.

Gharials hunt by waiting quietly in the water, with just their eyes and the tip of their snouts above the surface. When a fish swims past, they strike fast. They also catch their fish by sweeping their snouts from side to side in the water, feeling for movement. Their jaws are full of sensors that can detect the tiny movements of fish in the water, even when they cannot see them clearly.

## Eggs in the Sand

When it is time to lay eggs, a female gharial digs a deep hole in a sandy riverbank. She lays many eggs in the hole, covers them with sand, and then guards the nest. The warmth of the sun and the sand keeps the eggs warm, and after a couple of months, the baby gharials hatch.

The babies are small, about the length of a school ruler, and they are very cute. They have the same long thin snouts as their parents, just in miniature. The mother stays near the nest and helps the babies by carrying them gently to the water in her mouth. She does not eat them — she is very careful. Once they are in the water, the babies must fend for themselves, catching tiny fish and avoiding predators like birds and bigger fish.

## Why Gharials Are in Danger

Long ago, gharials were common in the rivers of India and Nepal. But their numbers fell sharply. People hunted them, and their river homes were changed by dams, sand mining and pollution. Fishing nets also caught gharials by accident. At one point, there were very few gharials left in the wild, and people worried that they might disappear altogether.

Thankfully, people stepped in to help. Special breeding centres were set up, where gharials are raised from eggs and then released into the rivers when they are big enough to survive. River sanctuaries were created to protect their homes. Today, gharial numbers are slowly growing again, but they are still rare, and they still need protection.

## A River Worth Protecting

Saving the gharial means saving the whole river. When a river is clean and healthy, with sandy banks and plenty of fish, gharials can thrive. And a healthy river is good for people too — for fishing, for farming, and for drinking water. The gharial is like a sign that tells us the river is doing well.

At Greennest Hub, we love the gharial because it is a gentle giant with a most unusual nose. It does not roar or chase big animals. It simply glides through the river, catching fish with its amazing snout, and basking in the sun on the sandbanks. If we look after the rivers, this wonderful animal will be swimming in them for many years to come.
