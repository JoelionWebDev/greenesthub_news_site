In the far north of the world, where snow covers the ground for most of the year and the sun barely rises in winter, lives a very special animal: the reindeer. Reindeer are tough, gentle, and strong, and for thousands of years, they have been the best friends of the people who live in the frozen Arctic. Reindeer pull sleds, carry loads, and provide food and warm clothing. Without reindeer, life in the far north would be very hard indeed. Let us journey to the frozen north and meet the reindeer!

## Meet the Reindeer

Reindeer are a kind of deer that live in the cold northern parts of the world — in Norway, Sweden, Finland, Russia, Canada and Alaska. In North America, wild reindeer are called caribou. They are the same animal, just with a different name!

Reindeer have thick, warm fur that keeps them cosy in freezing weather. Their fur has hollow hairs that trap air and act like a warm blanket. Their hooves are wide and flat, which helps them walk on soft snow without sinking — a bit like wearing snowshoes! In summer, the edges of their hooves become harder, which helps them grip the rocky ground.

Both male and female reindeer grow antlers — this is very unusual, because in most kinds of deer, only the males have antlers. A reindeer's antlers can be very large, with many branches. Reindeer shed their antlers every year and grow new ones. Male reindeer shed their antlers in winter, while females keep theirs through the winter, which helps them dig through the snow to find food.

Reindeer have a wonderful sense of smell. They can smell food buried under deep snow, and they use their hooves to dig through the snow to reach the plants underneath. Their favourite winter food is lichen, a strange plant that is part fungus and part algae. Lichen grows slowly on rocks and trees, and reindeer love to eat it.

## Reindeer and the People of the North

For thousands of years, the people of the far north — like the Sami people of Norway, Sweden and Finland, and the Nenets people of Russia — have lived closely with reindeer. These people are called reindeer herders. They look after herds of reindeer, moving with them across the tundra as the seasons change.

Reindeer herders and their reindeer travel together on long migrations. In summer, the herds move to cooler places where there are plenty of fresh plants to eat. In winter, they move to forests where the snow is not so deep and there is lichen to eat. The herders follow their reindeer on foot, on skis, or on sleds pulled by reindeer.

Reindeer have given northern people almost everything they need to survive. Their meat provides food. Their warm fur and hides are made into clothing, boots, tents and blankets. Their antlers and bones are carved into tools and ornaments. Even reindeer milk is used — it is very rich and creamy. For the people of the Arctic, the reindeer is not just an animal; it is a way of life.

## Sleds and Santa Claus

Reindeer are strong animals, and they have been used to pull sleds for hundreds of years. A reindeer sled is a wonderful way to travel across the snow. The reindeer is harnessed to the sled, and it pulls the sled and its passengers smoothly over the snow. Reindeer sleds were used to carry people, food, and supplies across the frozen north, long before snowmobiles were invented.

Of course, the most famous reindeer in the world are Santa Claus's reindeer! According to the beloved Christmas story, Santa's sleigh is pulled by nine reindeer, led by Rudolph with his bright red nose. The story comes from a poem written long ago, and it has become one of the most famous Christmas tales in the world. Children everywhere imagine Santa flying through the night sky behind his team of magical reindeer.

## The Great Migration

Wild reindeer and caribou make some of the longest journeys of any land animal. Every year, huge herds migrate hundreds of kilometres between their summer and winter homes. They travel in enormous groups — sometimes tens of thousands of animals together! The sight of a great caribou herd moving across the tundra is one of the wonders of the natural world.

During migration, reindeer cross rivers, climb hills, and walk for days without stopping for long. The calves are born during the migration, in the spring, and they can stand up and walk within hours of being born. They must keep up with the herd, or they will be left behind. It is a tough journey, but reindeer are built for it.

## Baby Reindeer

A baby reindeer is called a calf. Calves are born in late spring, when the snow is melting and fresh plants are starting to grow. A newborn calf is small and wobbly, but it grows very quickly. Within a few days, it can run and keep up with its mother.

Mother reindeer are very caring. They lick their calves clean, feed them with rich milk, and protect them from danger. Calves stay close to their mothers for the first year of their lives, learning how to find food, how to migrate, and how to survive in the cold.

## Protecting Reindeer and Their Homes

Today, reindeer face some challenges. The Arctic is warming up, which changes the snow and ice that reindeer depend on. Sometimes rain falls on the snow and freezes into a hard layer of ice, which makes it difficult for reindeer to dig through to their food. Roads, mines and towns also cut across the migration routes that reindeer have used for thousands of years.

Many people are working to protect reindeer. Scientists study reindeer to understand how they are coping with a changing climate. Reindeer herders pass down their knowledge to younger generations, so the old ways of living with reindeer are not lost. National parks protect the wild places where reindeer migrate.

Reindeer remind us that humans and animals can live together in partnership for thousands of years, each helping the other to survive. In the frozen north, where the winters are long and the snow is deep, the reindeer is more than just an animal — it is a friend, a helper, and a symbol of life in the Arctic.
