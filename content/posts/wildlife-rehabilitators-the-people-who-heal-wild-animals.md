Imagine a baby bird that has fallen from its nest, a fox with a hurt leg, or a hedgehog that is too weak to survive the winter. Who helps them? There is a special group of people who do exactly that. They are called wildlife rehabilitators, and their job is to care for injured, sick, or orphaned wild animals and help them get well enough to return to the wild.

## What Is a Wildlife Rehabilitator?

A wildlife rehabilitator is a person who is trained to care for wild animals that cannot survive on their own. When an animal is found injured or orphaned, it is brought to a rehabilitation centre. There, the rehabilitator checks the animal carefully, treats its injuries, and gives it food, warmth, and a safe place to rest.

Rehabilitators care for all kinds of animals. They might look after birds, hedgehogs, squirrels, foxes, badgers, deer, bats, and even snakes or frogs. Each animal needs different care. A baby bird needs to be fed very often, while a hedgehog needs a warm, quiet place to sleep. A fox with a broken leg needs rest and medicine, while a young deer needs space to practise running.

The most important rule of wildlife rehabilitation is that the animals are wild, and they must stay wild. Rehabilitators do not keep wild animals as pets, and they try not to let the animals get too used to people. That way, when the animal is released, it can live a normal wild life, find its own food, and stay safe from danger.

## How Animals Arrive at a Rescue Centre

Wild animals arrive at rescue centres in many ways. Sometimes a kind person finds an injured animal by the side of the road and brings it in. Sometimes a farmer finds a baby animal that has lost its mother. Sometimes an animal is found after a storm has damaged its home, or after it has been hurt by a car.

When an animal arrives, the rehabilitator gives it a careful health check. They look at its eyes, ears, fur or feathers, and legs. They check whether it is warm enough, whether it is hungry or thirsty, and whether it has any injuries. If the animal is very ill, a vet who specialises in wildlife will help treat it. The vet might give medicine, bandage a wound, or set a broken bone.

After the health check, the animal is given a safe, quiet place to rest. Wild animals can be very frightened by people and noise, so rehabilitators keep the centre calm and peaceful. They cover the animal's cage with a cloth to make it feel safe, and they handle the animal as little as possible. Rest is one of the best medicines for a frightened animal.

## Feeding Wild Animals

Feeding wild animals is a big part of a rehabilitator's job, and it is not as simple as it sounds. Each species eats different food. Baby birds need insects and special food given with tweezers. Hedgehogs eat insects, worms, and special hedgehog food. Fox cubs drink special milk that is made to be like their mother's milk. Squirrels eat nuts, seeds, and fruit.

Rehabilitators must be careful to feed animals the right food in the right amounts. Feeding a wild animal the wrong food can make it very ill. They also need to feed babies very often. Some baby birds need feeding every hour during the day, which means rehabilitators work long hours, starting early in the morning and finishing late at night.

## Getting Ready for Release

When an animal is healthy again, the rehabilitator must decide whether it is ready to go back to the wild. This is a big decision. The animal must be able to find food, move properly, and stay safe from predators. A bird must be able to fly well. A fox must be able to run fast. A hedgehog must be a good weight for the time of year.

Before release, many animals spend time in a large outdoor enclosure. This is a safe space where they can practise flying, running, climbing, or hunting, without the dangers of the wild. Rehabilitators watch them carefully to make sure they are ready. If an animal is not ready, it stays a little longer.

Release day is the best day of all. The rehabilitator takes the animal to a safe place, often near where it was found, and opens the cage door. The animal steps out, looks around, and then runs, hops, or flies away into the wild. It is a happy and emotional moment. The rehabilitator knows they have given that animal a second chance at life.

## Fun Facts

- A baby bird that falls from its nest should usually be put back in the nest if it is safe to do so, because its parents will still care for it.
- Hedgehogs are one of the animals most often helped by wildlife rehabilitators, especially young ones that are too small to survive winter.
- A fox cub's eyes do not open until it is about two weeks old, so very young cubs need round-the-clock care.
- Bats are often brought to rescue centres after being found on the ground, and rehabilitators feed them with tiny amounts of special food.
- The best reward for a wildlife rehabilitator is watching an animal run, hop, or fly away back into the wild.

## How You Can Help Wild Animals

You can help wild animals too. If you find an injured wild animal, do not try to care for it yourself. Instead, tell an adult, and contact a local wildlife rescue centre or vet. They will know what to do. Keep the animal in a quiet, dark box with air holes, and do not give it food or water unless a rescue centre tells you to.

Wildlife rehabilitators show us the very best of what people can do for animals. With patience, skill, and a lot of love, they give injured and orphaned wild animals a second chance. Every animal they release is a small victory for nature, and a reminder that when people and animals work together, wonderful things can happen.
