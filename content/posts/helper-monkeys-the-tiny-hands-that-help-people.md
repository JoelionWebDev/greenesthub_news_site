Imagine you could not use your arms or hands. How would you pick up a book, turn on a light, or scratch an itchy nose? For some people, this is everyday life. But they have a very special helper — a small, clever monkey called a capuchin. Helper monkeys are trained to do all sorts of tasks for people who cannot use their arms, and they become loyal friends as well as helpers. They are one of the most amazing examples of animals and humans working together.

## Meet the Capuchin Monkey

Capuchin monkeys are small monkeys from the forests of Central and South America. They are about the size of a small cat, with brown or black fur, a lighter face, and a long, curling tail. But the most important thing about a capuchin is its hands. Like humans, capuchins have fingers and thumbs that can grip things. They can pick up small objects, turn pages, open containers and press buttons. This makes them perfect helpers.

Capuchins are also very clever. They are known as one of the smartest monkeys, and they can learn many different tasks. In the wild, they use stones as tools to crack nuts, and they wash their food in water. They are curious, playful and quick to learn, which makes them good students when they are trained to help people.

## How Monkeys Learn to Help

Helper monkeys are trained by special organisations, such as Helping Hands in the United States. The training takes a long time — often several years. The monkeys start learning when they are young, and they are taught one task at a time.

First, the monkey learns simple things, like picking up an object and giving it to a person. Then it learns harder tasks, like opening a bottle, turning on a light switch, or putting a straw into a cup. The training is done with patience and rewards. When the monkey does the right thing, it gets a treat and lots of praise. The monkey learns that helping is fun and rewarding.

When the monkey is ready, it is matched with a person who needs help. The person and the monkey spend time together, learning to understand each other. The monkey moves into the person's home and becomes part of the family.

## A Day with a Helper Monkey

A helper monkey's day is busy. In the morning, it might help its person get out of bed, fetch a toothbrush, or turn on the television. During the day, it can pick up things that have been dropped, bring a book or a phone, open a door, or switch on a light. It can even help with eating, by putting food on a spoon or holding a cup steady.

The monkey does all this with its clever hands and its long tail. Its tail is strong and can grip things too, almost like a fifth hand. The monkey lives in the person's home, usually in its own special area, and it is looked after with great care. It eats a healthy diet of fruits, vegetables, nuts and special monkey food, and it gets regular check-ups from a vet.

Helper monkeys are not just workers — they are companions too. Many people who have a helper monkey say that the monkey becomes their best friend. The monkey keeps them company, makes them laugh with its playful tricks, and gives them comfort when they are sad.

## Who Can Have a Helper Monkey?

Helper monkeys help people who have lost the use of their arms, for example after a spinal cord injury or an illness. These are people who cannot move their arms or hands, but who can still live at home with the right support. A helper monkey gives them more independence. Instead of waiting for someone to come and help, they can ask their monkey to fetch something or switch on a light.

Not everyone can have a helper monkey. The monkeys are carefully trained, and there are not many of them, so they go to the people who need them most. The person must be able to look after the monkey properly, with help from family or carers, and must have a suitable home. It is a big commitment, for both the person and the monkey, and it can last for many years.

## The Friendship Between Monkeys and People

The bond between a helper monkey and its person is very special. The monkey learns to read its person's voice and facial expressions. It knows when its person is happy, sad or in need of help. The person, in turn, learns to understand the monkey's moods and needs. They become a team.

This friendship is good for the monkey too. In the wild, capuchins live in groups and are very social. A helper monkey lives closely with its person and family, and it gets lots of attention, play and care. The training organisations make sure that the monkeys are happy and healthy, and that they are treated kindly.

## Why Helper Monkeys Are Special

Helper monkeys show us just how clever animals can be, and how much they can do for us. A small monkey with hands like ours can give a person independence, friendship and joy.
