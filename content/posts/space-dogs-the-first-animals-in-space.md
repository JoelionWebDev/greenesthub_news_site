# Space Dogs: The First Animals in Space

Long before the first person travelled into space, the first travellers were not people at all. They were dogs. In the 1950s, when rockets were still new and nobody knew if living things could survive in space, scientists sent dogs on daring journeys above the Earth. These brave dogs helped people learn how to travel safely into space, and they opened the way for human astronauts.

## Why Dogs?

You might wonder why scientists chose dogs for these first space journeys. There are good reasons. Dogs are clever, calm and friendly. They can be trained to sit quietly in a small space, to wear a harness, and to stay still for a long time. They are also about the same size as a small person, and their bodies work in similar ways to ours in many respects. This meant that what scientists learned from the dogs would help them understand how a human body might cope in space.

The dogs chosen for space were mostly small, mixed-breed dogs, often found as strays in the streets of Moscow in Russia. The scientists believed that street dogs were tough and clever, because they had learned to survive on their own. They were also small and light, which was important, because rockets could only carry a small amount of weight.

Before their flights, the dogs were given special training. They learned to live in a small capsule, to eat special food from tubes, and to wear a space suit. They also practiced sitting in a shaking machine, which felt like the shaking of a rocket taking off. The dogs who stayed calm and did well in training were chosen to fly.

## Laika: The First Animal in Orbit

The most famous space dog of all was a little dog called Laika. She was a small, quiet dog with pointed ears, found as a stray in Moscow. On 3 November 1957, she became the first animal to orbit the Earth, travelling aboard a spacecraft called Sputnik 2.

Sputnik 2 was launched by the Soviet Union, the country that is now Russia. At that time, the space race was beginning, and countries were competing to be the first to send things and people into space. Sputnik 2 carried Laika in a small cabin with food, water and air, and instruments that sent information about her back to Earth.

Laika's journey was a great achievement for science, but it was also a sad story, and we should be honest about it. The scientists who sent her knew that the technology to bring her home did not yet exist. She died during the flight, only a few hours after launch. She did not come back to Earth.

Today, people remember Laika with kindness. There is a statue of her in Moscow, and her picture appears on stamps and in books. Her flight taught scientists a great deal about how a living body behaves in space, and it showed that living things could survive the launch and the journey into orbit. But it also taught people that animals should not be sent on journeys they cannot return from, and space travel is much kinder to animals today.

## Belka and Strelka: The Dogs Who Came Home

After Laika's flight, scientists worked hard to build spacecraft that could come back safely. Three years later, in 1960, two more dogs had their turn. Their names were Belka and Strelka, which mean "white" and "arrow" in Russian. Belka was a small white dog, and Strelka was a little darker.

On 19 August 1960, Belka and Strelka blasted off aboard a spacecraft called Sputnik 5. They orbited the Earth many times during their journey, which lasted about a day. This time, the spacecraft was designed to come back, and it did. Belka and Strelka landed safely back on Earth, healthy and happy.

People all over the world cheered when they heard the news. Belka and Strelka had proved that living things could go into space and come back safely. Their success was a huge step towards sending the first human into space, which happened less than a year later, when the cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin orbited the Earth in 1961.

Strelka later became a mother, and one of her puppies was given as a gift to the family of the president of the United States. It was a sign of friendship, and a reminder that the space dogs belonged to the whole world, not just one country.

## Other Space Dogs

Laika, Belka and Strelka were not the only dogs to fly into space. Many other dogs made journeys in rockets, some going high above the Earth and coming back safely, others flying on shorter trips. Dogs called Dezik and Tsygan were among the first to fly in rockets in the early 1950s, and they came home safely. Another dog, called Zvezdochka, which means "little star", flew just a few weeks before Yuri Gagarin's famous flight, to test that the spacecraft was safe for a person.

## What Did We Learn From the Space Dogs?

The space dogs taught scientists many important lessons. They showed that a living body could survive the powerful shaking and noise of a rocket launch. They showed that animals could live and move in weightlessness. And Belka and Strelka showed that a journey into space could end with a safe return home.

## Remembering the Space Dogs

The space dogs are remembered in many ways. Laika has a statue in Moscow, and her name is known around the world. Belka and Strelka are also remembered in books, films and museums. Some of the space dogs are preserved in museums, where visitors can learn about their brave journeys.

## Fun Facts About the Space Dogs

- Laika was the first animal to orbit the Earth, aboard Sputnik 2 in 1957.
- Belka and Strelka orbited the Earth in 1960 and returned safely.
- Belka means "white" and Strelka means "arrow" in Russian.
- The first human in space, Yuri Gagarin, flew less than a year after Belka and Strelka.
- Strelka later had puppies, and one was given as a gift to the president of the United States.
- There is a statue of Laika in Moscow.

The space dogs were small, brave and clever, and they travelled farther than any dog had ever travelled before. Their journeys opened the door to space for all of us, and their story is one of the most remarkable adventures in history.
