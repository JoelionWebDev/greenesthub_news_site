Long ago, before there were cars or phones or even cameras, people in many parts of the world learned to hunt together with birds. This is called falconry. A falconer is a person who trains a bird of prey, such as a falcon, a hawk, or an eagle, to fly free and then return. The bird is not kept in a cage like a pet. It is a wild animal that chooses to come back to its falconer because it trusts them. Falconry is one of the oldest partnerships between people and animals in the world, and it is still practised today.

## A Very Old Friendship

Falconry began thousands of years ago. Historians believe it first started in Asia, in places like Mongolia and China, and then spread to the Middle East and Europe. Kings, queens, and nobles loved falconry, and it became a popular sport among rich and powerful people. In medieval times, falconry was so important that different kinds of birds were linked to different ranks of people. But ordinary people practised it too, and in some places it was a way to catch food for their families.

Today, falconry is recognised around the world as an important part of human culture. It is not just a sport. It is a way of life that teaches patience, respect, and a deep understanding of nature. Falconers must learn a great deal about birds before they can ever fly one. They study how birds hunt, how they rest, and how they behave in different kinds of weather. A good falconer knows their bird as well as a best friend.

## The Birds of Falconry

The birds used in falconry are called birds of prey. They are hunters, with sharp beaks, strong talons, and eyesight that is far better than ours. A falcon can spot a tiny animal from high in the sky, and dive down at amazing speed. The peregrine falcon is the fastest animal in the world when it dives, and it can swoop down faster than a racing car drives. Hawks are quick and clever, darting between trees to catch their prey. Eagles are big and powerful, with strong wings that can carry them high above the mountains.

Each bird has its own personality. Some are bold and confident. Others are shy and take longer to trust their falconer. Falconers choose a bird that suits their experience and the land where they live. In many places, falconers work with birds that have been bred in captivity, or with young birds that need care. The bird is always treated with great respect, because without trust, falconry simply does not work.

## Training a Bird

Training a bird of prey is very different from training a dog. You cannot teach a falcon to sit or fetch. Instead, the falconer slowly builds trust. At first, the bird wears a soft leather hood over its head to keep it calm. The falconer offers the bird food from their gloved hand, and the bird learns that the falconer is a safe person who brings food. Over time, the hood comes off for longer and longer, and the bird learns to fly to the falconer when called.

The falconer wears a thick leather glove on one hand, because the bird's sharp talons could hurt bare skin. The bird perches on the glove, and when the falconer raises their arm, the bird flies off into the sky. It circles high above, searching for prey below. When the falconer calls or shows a lure, which is a piece of food swung on a line, the bird swoops back down. It is a breathtaking moment to watch a falcon return from the clouds, folding its wings and landing softly on the glove.

Training takes many months, and even then the falconer must keep practising every day. The bird must stay fit and healthy, with the right amount of food and exercise. If a bird is too full, it will not want to fly. If it is too hungry, it will not have the strength to hunt. A good falconer knows exactly how to care for their bird.

## A Day Out with a Falcon

A day of falconry often starts early in the morning. The falconer checks the weather, because birds fly best on clear, calm days. Then they drive or walk out to open fields or hills. The bird is carried on the glove, wearing its hood. When they reach the right place, the falconer removes the hood and lets the bird fly.

The bird soars up into the sky, circling higher and higher until it is just a small dot. The falconer watches it carefully, sometimes using a small radio tracker to know where it is. When the bird spots something to chase, it folds its wings and dives. It is one of the most exciting sights in nature. Afterwards, the bird returns to the glove for a reward. The falconer and the bird may fly together for a few hours before heading home.

In the evening, the bird rests in a special shelter called a mews. The mews is a clean, quiet room where the bird can sleep safely. The falconer checks the bird's feathers, feet, and weight, and makes sure it has fresh water. Caring for a bird of prey is a big responsibility, and falconers take it very seriously.

## Falconry Today

Today, falconry is still practised in many countries. Some falconers fly their birds for sport, while others use their birds to help keep other birds away from airports, farms, and cities. A trained falcon can scare away flocks of birds that might be dangerous near aeroplanes. This is a very useful job, and it helps to keep people safe.

Falconry also helps to protect wild birds. Falconers learn a great deal about birds of prey, and many of them help with breeding programmes and conservation work. They care deeply about the wild places where these birds live, and they work to protect them.

If you ever get the chance to watch a falconry display, take it. You will see a bird fly free in the sky and return to a human hand, a partnership built on trust. It is a reminder that people and animals can work together, and that some friendships are thousands of years old and still going strong.
