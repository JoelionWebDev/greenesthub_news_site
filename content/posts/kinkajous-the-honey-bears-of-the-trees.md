High up in the rainforest trees, where the flowers bloom and the fruit hangs sweet and ripe, lives a small, furry animal with big round eyes and a very long tongue. People call it the kinkajou, and some people call it the honey bear. It is not really a bear at all, but it loves honey so much that the name stuck. Let's climb up into the trees and meet this delightful night-time animal.

## Not a Bear, Not a Monkey

The kinkajou is a strange and wonderful animal. It belongs to the same family as raccoons, which means its closest relatives are raccoons, not bears or monkeys. But when you look at a kinkajou, you can see why people get confused. It has soft, golden-brown fur, a round face, and big, gentle eyes. It moves slowly through the trees, using its tail like an extra hand.

Kinkajous live in the rainforests of Central and South America. They spend almost their whole lives in the trees, rarely coming down to the ground. The forest canopy, which is the top layer of the rainforest, is their home. Up there, they find food, shelter, and safety from animals that hunt on the ground.

A kinkajou is about the size of a house cat, but its tail is very long, sometimes as long as its body. This tail is special. It is prehensile, which means the kinkajou can use it to grip branches, just like a hand. The kinkajou can hang from a branch by its tail alone, leaving its front paws free to grab food. Imagine being able to hang upside down from a tree by your tail while you eat dinner. That is everyday life for a kinkajou.

## A Tongue Made for Sweet Things

The kinkajou's most famous feature is its tongue. It is very long, and the kinkajou can stick it out far beyond its mouth. This long tongue is perfect for reaching deep inside flowers to lick up nectar. Nectar is the sweet juice that flowers make, and kinkajous love it. They also love honey, which is why people call them honey bears.

When a kinkajou visits a flower, it does more than just enjoy a sweet snack. As it drinks the nectar, pollen from the flower sticks to its fur. When the kinkajou visits another flower, the pollen rubs off, and the flower can make seeds. This means kinkajous help flowers grow and spread, just like bees and butterflies do. Kinkajous are pollinators, and the rainforest needs them.

Kinkajous eat many other things besides nectar and honey. They love ripe fruit, especially figs, mangoes, and bananas. They also eat flowers, leaves, and sometimes insects. They use their long tongue to lick fruit pulp and to reach into tight spaces where food might be hiding. Their teeth are not very sharp, because they do not need to tear meat. They are built for soft, sweet food.

At night, when the rainforest is dark and quiet, kinkajous wake up and start to look for food. They move slowly and carefully through the branches, sniffing the air for the smell of ripe fruit or sweet flowers. Their sense of smell is very good, and it helps them find food in the dark.

## Life in the Dark

Kinkajous are nocturnal, which means they are active at night and sleep during the day. During the day, they curl up in a safe hiding place, such as a hollow tree or a tangle of branches. They wrap their long tail around their body like a blanket and sleep soundly until the sun goes down.

Being active at night has many advantages. The forest is cooler at night, and there are fewer predators around. Many of the flowers that kinkajous love open at night, filling the air with sweet smells. The night is also quieter, so kinkajous can hear the sounds of the forest and stay alert for danger.

Kinkajous have large, round eyes that help them see in the dark. Their eyes gather as much light as possible, so they can move safely through the trees even when the moon is hidden behind clouds. Their ears are small and round, and they can hear the tiny sounds of insects and other animals moving in the dark.

Kinkajous are not fast animals. They move slowly and carefully, which helps them stay quiet and avoid being noticed. If a kinkajou senses danger, it will freeze and stay very still, blending into the leaves. Its golden-brown fur matches the colour of tree bark and dry leaves, making it hard to spot.

## A Gentle and Social Animal

Unlike many rainforest animals, kinkajous are quite social. They sometimes live in small groups, and they enjoy playing together. Young kinkajous love to play, chasing each other through the branches and wrestling gently. They also groom each other, which means they clean each other's fur with their tongues and paws. Grooming helps kinkajous stay clean, and it also helps them bond with each other.

Mother kinkajous take great care of their babies. A baby kinkajou is born in a safe den in a hollow tree. At first, the baby is tiny and helpless, and it stays close to its mother. The mother carries her baby in her mouth or on her back as she moves through the trees. She feeds it milk and keeps it warm.

As the baby grows, it starts to explore. It learns to climb by watching its mother and copying what she does. It practises gripping branches with its tail and reaching for fruit with its paws. The mother teaches her baby which fruits are safe to eat and where to find the sweetest flowers. Young kinkajous stay with their mother for a long time, learning all the skills they need to live in the trees.

Kinkajous make many different sounds. They can bark, squeak, and make soft, purring noises. They use these sounds to talk to each other, especially at night when they cannot see each other well. A mother kinkajou might call to her baby with a soft chirp, and the baby will answer back.

If you ever visit a rainforest, look up into the trees at night. You might see a kinkajou moving slowly through the branches, its long tail wrapped around a limb, its tongue reaching into a flower. It is a magical sight, and one you will never forget.
