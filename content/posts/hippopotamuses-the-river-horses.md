The word "hippopotamus" comes from an old Greek word that means "river horse." It is a perfect name, because hippos spend most of their lives in rivers and lakes, just like horses graze in fields. But do not be fooled by their sleepy, lazy look — hippos are one of the most powerful animals in Africa. They have enormous mouths, giant teeth, and a surprising secret: even though they look clumsy on land, they can move very fast when they need to. Let us dive into the world of the river horse!

## Meet the Hippo

A hippopotamus is a huge animal. An adult hippo can weigh as much as a small car, and its body is round and barrel-shaped, with short, stubby legs. Its skin is smooth and mostly grey or brownish-purple. Hippos have tiny ears, small eyes and nostrils that sit on top of their heads, like the top of a submarine's periscope. This is very useful, because a hippo can float in the water with just its eyes, ears and nose poking out, while the rest of its body stays hidden below the surface.

Hippos are excellent swimmers, but here is a surprise: they cannot actually float very well, and they cannot swim in the way a fish does! Their bodies are so heavy that they sink. Instead of swimming, hippos walk or bounce along the bottom of the river, pushing off with their feet. They can hold their breath underwater for several minutes, and they even close their ears and nostrils to keep water out. Baby hippos can suckle milk from their mothers underwater — they close their ears and nostrils, just like their mums do.

A hippo's mouth is enormous. When it opens its jaws wide, the gap can be huge — wide enough to fit a whole child inside, though hippos would never want to eat a child! Inside that mouth are giant teeth. The biggest teeth, called canine teeth, can grow very long and sharp. Hippos use these teeth to fight other hippos, not to eat. Hippos are vegetarians, so their big teeth are for defence, not dinner.

## Life in the Water

Hippos spend their days in the water, and for a very good reason: their skin needs to stay wet. A hippo's skin is very sensitive to the sun. If it stays out of the water in the hot African sun, its skin can dry out and get sunburnt. The water keeps the hippo cool and protects its skin.

Hippos also make their own sunscreen! Their skin produces a pinkish, oily liquid that looks a little like blood. People used to think hippos sweated blood, but it is not blood at all. This special liquid works like a natural sunscreen and also helps protect the hippo's skin from germs. Is that not clever?

During the day, groups of hippos rest together in the river, floating, dozing, and sometimes snorting and grunting at each other. A group of hippos is called a pod, and a pod can have many hippos in it. They like to stay close together in the water, where it is cool and safe. Sometimes only their heads stick out of the water, like a row of grey boulders with eyes.

## Dinner Time on Land

At night, when the sun goes down and the air is cool, hippos leave the water and go out to eat. They walk along paths they know well, out onto the grasslands near the river. Hippos eat grass — lots and lots of grass. An adult hippo can eat a huge amount of grass in a single night, using its wide, flat lips to pull up mouthfuls of grass like a lawnmower.

Hippos usually graze alone or in small groups, and they can walk several kilometres from the river in one night. They always follow the same paths, which become worn trails in the grass. Before the sun rises, they hurry back to the water, where they will spend the whole day resting again.

## The Grumpy Side of Hippos

Hippos may look lazy and slow, but they are actually one of the most dangerous animals in Africa. Male hippos are very territorial, which means they protect their part of the river fiercely. If another male hippo comes too close, there can be a big fight. The males open their huge mouths wide to show off their long teeth, and they may charge at each other. These fights can be very serious, because those teeth are sharp and powerful.

Hippos are also very protective of their babies, and mothers will charge at anything that comes too close to their calf. That is why people are always told to keep a safe distance from hippos, especially in the water. Hippos are not friendly pets — they are wild animals that deserve respect and space.

Even though they look heavy and slow on land, hippos can run surprisingly fast for a short distance. So if you ever visit Africa, the best place to watch a hippo is from far away, with binoculars, from a safe spot on the riverbank.

## Where Do Hippos Live?

Hippos live in sub-Saharan Africa, in countries south of the Sahara Desert. They live in rivers, lakes and swamps, anywhere there is plenty of water and grass nearby. Long ago, hippos also lived in North Africa, including Egypt, where ancient Egyptians saw them in the River Nile. Today, they are found only further south.

The closest living relative of the hippopotamus is not the horse or the pig, as you might guess — it is the whale! Scientists discovered that hippos and whales share an ancestor that lived millions of years ago. Is that not amazing? The river horse and the ocean whale are cousins!

## Protecting the River Horses

Today, hippos face threats. Their rivers and lakes are sometimes polluted or dried up, and their grasslands are being turned into farms. Sometimes hippos are hunted for their meat and their ivory teeth. But many people are working to protect them. National parks in Africa keep hippos safe, and rangers watch over the rivers where they live.

Hippos remind us that looks can be deceiving. They seem slow and sleepy, floating all day in the river — but they are strong, fast, protective parents, and very important to the rivers they call home. The next time you see a picture of a hippo with just its eyes and ears peeking out of the water, remember: underneath the surface is one of Africa's most powerful animals, the mighty river horse.
