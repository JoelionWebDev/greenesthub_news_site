Far away in the Indian Ocean lies the island of Madagascar, a place full of animals that live nowhere else on Earth. At the top of the food chain on this island is a creature called the fossa. It looks a bit like a cat, a bit like a dog and a bit like a mongoose, but it is none of those. It is its own special animal, and it is the largest hunter in Madagascar. Lemurs, the famous leaping primates of the island, watch out for it day and night, because the fossa is the one animal they fear most.

## What Exactly Is a Fossa?

Scientists took a long time to work out what the fossa is. It has a long body, short legs, a small head with round ears, and a very long tail. Its fur is a reddish-brown colour, and its belly is lighter. It is about the size of a medium dog, but much slimmer and more graceful.

The fossa is related to the mongoose family, which might surprise you, because it looks so much like a cat. But animals do not always look like their relatives. The fossa's ancestors arrived in Madagascar a very long time ago, and over many years they changed and grew bigger, becoming the island's top hunter. This is a wonderful example of how animals can change to fit their homes.

Its long tail is almost as long as its body. The tail is not just for looks — it helps the fossa balance when it climbs and leaps through the trees. It works like the long pole a tightrope walker uses to stay steady.

## A Hunter in the Trees

Fossas are brilliant climbers. They can run along branches, leap from tree to tree, and climb down a tree trunk headfirst, which is something very few animals can do. Their paws have sharp claws that grip the bark, and the pads on their feet are soft and flexible, almost like little hands. This helps them hold on to branches as they move.

Their favourite food is lemurs. Lemurs are clever, quick animals that live in the trees, so catching one is not easy. The fossa hunts both in the trees and on the ground. It can chase a lemur through the branches, or wait quietly below and pounce when the lemur comes down. Fossas also eat birds, small mammals, reptiles and insects. They are not fussy — if it moves and they can catch it, they will try.

Fossas hunt mostly during the day and at dusk, when the light is soft. They move quietly, using their excellent sense of smell and their sharp eyes to find food. They can cover a large area of forest in a single day, patrolling their home range in search of a meal.

## Life Alone in the Forest

Unlike lions or wolves, which hunt in groups, the fossa lives alone. Each fossa has its own area of forest, and it does not like sharing it with other fossas. Males have bigger areas than females, and their areas may overlap with those of a few females. They mark their area with scent, leaving messages for other fossas to stay away.

Fossas are shy animals. They spend most of their lives high in the trees or hidden in the thick forest, and they are rarely seen by people. Even scientists who study them find them hard to follow. They sleep in hollow trees or in caves during the hottest part of the day, then wake up to hunt.

When two fossas do meet, they make a range of sounds. They can purr like a cat, growl when they are angry, and make a sharp yapping sound to call to each other. Young fossas stay with their mothers for a long time, learning how to climb and hunt, before they go off to find their own forest home.

## Baby Fossas

Female fossas give birth to their babies in a safe den, often in a hollow tree or a rocky cave. The babies are born blind and helpless, with soft grey fur. Their eyes open after a couple of weeks, and they grow quickly on their mother's milk.

After a few months, the young fossas start to explore. They practise climbing on low branches, copying their mother. They play and wrestle with each other, which helps them learn the moves they will need for hunting. By the time they are about a year old, they are ready to leave and find their own place in the forest.

## Madagascar, the Island of Wonders

Madagascar is a very special place. Because it split away from Africa millions of years ago, the animals there evolved on their own, cut off from the rest of the world. That is why so many of Madagascar's animals, like lemurs, fossas and chameleons, live nowhere else. The island is like a natural laboratory, full of creatures you cannot find anywhere else on the planet.

But Madagascar's forests are shrinking. Trees are cut down for farming and wood, and the animals that live there lose their homes. The fossa needs a large area of forest to hunt in, so when the forest gets smaller, the fossa suffers. People in Madagascar and around the world are working to protect the forests, so that fossas, lemurs and all the other special animals of the island can survive.

## Why the Fossa Matters

The fossa is important because it keeps the forest in balance. By hunting lemurs and other animals, it stops any one animal from becoming too common. A forest with a healthy fossa population is a forest that is working properly.

The fossa also reminds us that there are still mysterious animals in the world. It took scientists a long time to understand it, and even today, there is much we still do not know about how it lives. At Greennest Hub, we love the fossa because it is a puzzle of an animal — part cat, part mongoose, all hunter — and it rules the forests of one of the most amazing islands on Earth.
