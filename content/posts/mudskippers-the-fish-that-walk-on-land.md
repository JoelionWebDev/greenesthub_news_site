Meet the mudskipper, a fish that walks on land, climbs trees and breathes air. It lives in muddy shores where the sea meets the land. If you have ever seen a fish hop across the mud like a frog, you have probably seen a mudskipper. These funny little fish break all the rules about what a fish should do, and they are one of the most amazing creatures of the seashore.

## A Fish Out of Water

Mudskippers live in mangrove swamps and muddy shores in warm parts of the world, such as West Africa, Southeast Asia and northern Australia. A mangrove swamp is a place where the sea washes in and out twice a day, leaving behind soft, wet mud. Most fish would be in big trouble when the water goes out. But not the mudskipper. When the tide goes out, the mudskipper simply stays behind on the mud and carries on with its day.

Mudskippers can breathe air, which is very unusual for a fish. They take a mouthful of water and hold it in their gill chambers, like carrying a little bottle of water with them. As long as their gills stay wet, they can breathe. They can also breathe through their skin, as long as it stays damp. That is why you will see mudskippers sitting in shallow puddles or rolling in the wet mud to keep their skin moist.

They have big eyes that stick up on top of their heads, like two little periscopes. These eyes help them watch for danger and spot food while they sit on the mud. They can even pull their eyes down into their heads to keep them wet, which looks very funny.

## Walking, Hopping and Climbing

Mudskippers do not swim very much. Instead, they move around on land using their strong front fins, which work a bit like little arms. They push themselves along the mud, hop from place to place, and can even climb up mangrove roots and low tree trunks. Some mudskippers can climb quite high into the trees.

When a mudskipper wants to move quickly, it flips its tail and hops, a bit like a frog. It can hop from one puddle to another, or skip across the mud to catch a tasty insect. Watching a group of mudskippers hopping around the mudflat is one of the funniest sights in nature.

Their bodies are made for this strange life. They are a muddy brown or greenish colour, which helps them hide on the mud. Some kinds have bright blue or green spots, and the males can be very colourful when they want to impress a female.

## What Do Mudskippers Eat?

Mudskippers eat small creatures that live in the mud and on the shore. They love tiny crabs, worms, insects and other small animals. They hunt by hopping around and snapping up anything small that moves. They are quick and clever hunters, and their big eyes help them spot food from far away.

At low tide, when the water is gone, the mudflat is full of food. Mudskippers take advantage of this, feeding busily while other fish are stuck waiting for the tide to come back. When the tide returns, the mudskippers swim a little, but they are happiest when they can hop about on the mud.

## The Mudskipper's Little House

Male mudskippers are hard workers. They dig burrows in the mud, which are like little underground houses. The burrow has a tunnel that goes down into the mud, and the male keeps it clean and safe. When a female is ready to lay her eggs, she lays them inside the burrow, on the walls and ceiling.

The male stays and guards the eggs. He fills the burrow with fresh air by taking big gulps of air at the surface and carrying the air down into the burrow in his mouth. He does this again and again, so the eggs have enough air to grow. It is a lot of work, but he keeps at it until the eggs hatch.

When the tiny baby mudskippers hatch, they swim out into the water. They start their lives as normal little fish, swimming in the sea. As they grow, they slowly learn to spend more and more time on the mud, until they become mudskippers just like their parents.

## Fights and Dances

Male mudskippers can be very proud and a little bit bossy. They defend their patch of mud from other males, and they do this by showing off. A male will raise his back fin high, open his mouth wide and hop up and down in front of his rival. It looks like a dance, and it usually scares the other male away without a real fight.

They also show off to impress females, waving their fins and hopping about. The brightest and bravest males get the best spots on the mudflat and the most attention from the females.

## Why Mudskippers Matter

Mudskippers are important because they show us how animals can adapt to live in two worlds at once. They are fish that have learned to live on land, just as the very first animals that left the sea did, millions of years ago. Scientists study mudskippers to learn about how life on land began.

Mudskippers also need healthy mangrove forests to live in. Mangroves are special trees that grow in salty water along the coast, and they protect the shore from storms and give homes to many animals. When mangroves are cut down, mudskippers lose their homes. People are working to protect mangrove forests, so mudskippers and all the other animals that live there can stay safe.

## A Fish Like No Other

The mudskipper is a wonderful reminder that nature loves to surprise us. A fish that walks, a fish that climbs trees, a fish that breathes air — it sounds impossible, but it is real, and it is hopping about on muddy shores right now.

So next time you are near the sea, look closely at the mud. You might just spot a pair of little eyes poking up, and a funny fish hopping past. That is the mudskipper, the fish that decided the land was worth a visit, and never went back to the water for good.
