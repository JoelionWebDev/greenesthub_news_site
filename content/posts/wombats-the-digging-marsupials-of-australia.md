## Meet the Wombat

Deep in the grasslands and forests of Australia lives a short, sturdy animal that looks a bit like a small bear mixed with a guinea pig. It has a big head, a wide nose, short legs, and a tiny stub of a tail. Its name is the wombat, and it is one of Australia's most unusual animals.

Wombats are marsupials. That means they are related to kangaroos and koalas, and mother wombats carry their babies in a pouch, just like kangaroos do. But a wombat's pouch is very special, and we will find out why in a moment.

There are a few kinds of wombats, but they all share the same chunky shape and the same love of digging. They are mostly active at night, when the air is cool, and they spend their days resting in burrows underground. A wombat might look slow and sleepy, but it is a strong and clever little engineer.

## The Champion Diggers

Wombats are amazing diggers. Their front paws have strong, flat claws that work like little shovels. With those claws, a wombat can dig a burrow that is long, deep, and full of twists and turns. Some burrows have more than one entrance and more than one sleeping room inside.

Why do wombats dig so much? Their burrows keep them safe and comfortable. Underground, it is cool on a hot day and warm on a cold night. The burrow also protects them from animals that might want to catch them. When danger comes near, a wombat can hurry home and dive into its burrow.

Wombats also have a very clever trick for staying safe in their burrows. The back of a wombat is very tough, made of thick skin and bone. If a hunter tries to follow a wombat into its burrow, the wombat can block the tunnel with its hard bottom, like closing a door. It can even use its bottom to squash an intruder against the tunnel roof. It is a strange trick, but it works!

## A Pouch That Faces Backwards

Here is the wombat's most famous feature: its pouch faces backwards. Most marsupials, like kangaroos, have a pouch that opens at the top, facing forwards. A baby kangaroo peeks out of its mother's pouch and looks ahead.

A wombat's pouch opens towards the mother's back legs, facing backwards. Why? Because wombats dig! If the pouch faced forwards, every time the mother dug a burrow, dirt would fly into the pouch and cover the baby. With a backwards pouch, the baby stays clean and safe while the mother digs away.

It is a perfect example of how an animal's body is made to fit its way of life. The wombat digs, so its pouch faces the other way. Nature is very clever like that.

## What Do Wombats Eat?

Wombats are herbivores, which means they eat only plants. Their favourite food is grass, but they also eat leaves, roots, and bark. They use their sharp front teeth to bite off grass, and their strong jaws to grind it up.

Wombats are slow eaters in one way, and fast eaters in another. They munch their food slowly and carefully, but they can eat a lot of grass in one night. Their teeth never stop growing, so all that chewing helps keep their teeth the right size.

One of the most surprising things about wombats is how little water they need. They get most of the water they need from the plants they eat. This helps them live in dry parts of Australia, where water can be hard to find.

## Baby Wombats

A baby wombat is called a joey, just like a baby kangaroo. When a joey is born, it is tiny, pink, and has no fur. It cannot see or walk yet. It crawls into its mother's pouch and stays there, drinking milk and growing bigger and stronger.

The joey stays in the pouch for several months. As it grows, it starts to peek out, first with its little head, and then with more of its body. Eventually it is too big for the pouch, and it starts to follow its mother around outside the burrow.

## Wombat Droppings: Square Poo!

Now for the funniest fact about wombats: their poo is square! Wombats are the only animals known to make cube-shaped droppings. It sounds silly, but there is a good reason for it.

Wombats use their droppings to mark their territory, a bit like putting up a sign that says, "This is my home." Square droppings do not roll away, so they stay right where the wombat left them, on top of rocks or logs where other wombats can see and smell them.

How does a wombat make square poo? The inside of its gut is stretchy in some places and stiff in others, and as the food moves through, it gets shaped into little cubes.

## Staying Safe in the Wild

Life is not always easy for a wombat. They share their home with animals like dingoes and eagles, and they need to be careful. Their best plan is to stay near their burrow and to be ready to run home at the first sign of trouble.

Wombats can run surprisingly fast when they need to, even though their legs are short. They can also climb over rocks and push through thick bushes. And if they do get caught, their tough skin and strong body make them hard to hurt.

## Fun Facts

- Wombats are marsupials, related to kangaroos and koalas.
- Their pouch faces backwards, so dirt does not fly in while the mother digs.
- Wombats are champion diggers, with strong claws like little shovels.
- They can block their burrow with their tough bottom to keep intruders out.
- Wombats eat grass, leaves, roots, and bark.
- Their teeth never stop growing, and chewing grass keeps them worn down.
- Baby wombats are called joeys and stay in the pouch for months.
- Wombats make square-shaped droppings, the only animals known to do so.
- Square droppings do not roll away, so they make good territory markers.
- Wombats get most of their water from the plants they eat.

## Australia's Little Engineer

Wombats may not be as famous as kangaroos or koalas, but they are just as special. They are diggers, builders, and survivors, with a backwards pouch and a tough little body.

Next time you think of Australia, remember the wombat, digging its burrow and making its home in one of the toughest landscapes on Earth.
