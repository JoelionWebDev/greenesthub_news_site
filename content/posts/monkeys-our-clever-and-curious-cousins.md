Have you ever watched a monkey at the zoo and felt like it was watching you right back? That is because monkeys are some of the animals most like us. They have hands that can hold things, faces that show feelings, and brains that love to learn. They laugh and play, they look after their babies, and they can solve tricky problems. Monkeys live in warm places all over the world, from the thick forests of Africa and Asia to the jungles of South America. People have loved watching them for a very long time, because every time we watch a monkey, we learn a little more about ourselves too.

## Meet the monkey family

There are many kinds of monkeys, and they come in all shapes and sizes. Some are tiny enough to sit in the palm of your hand, while others are as big as a small child. Some have long tails that work like an extra hand, helping them swing from branch to branch. Others have short tails or no tail at all. Monkeys can have bright faces, fluffy fur, and long arms made for climbing. Some live high up in the trees and almost never come down to the ground. Others spend much of their time on the forest floor, walking, running and searching for food. Wherever they live, monkeys are built for a life of climbing, jumping and exploring.

## Clever hands and clever minds

A monkey's hands are one of its greatest gifts. Like our hands, they have fingers and a thumb that can grip things. This helps a monkey hold food, peel a banana, pick up a stone, or hold on tight to a branch. Monkeys use tools too. Some use stones to crack open hard nuts. Others use sticks to reach tasty food that is hidden away. Some even wash their food in water before they eat it. Scientists who study monkeys have seen them learn new tricks by watching each other. A young monkey watches its mother, copies what she does, and soon learns the trick itself. That is a lot like how children learn at school and at home.

## Life in a monkey troop

Monkeys almost never live alone. They live in big family groups called troops, and a troop can have many monkeys in it. In a troop, everyone has a job to do. The grown-ups look after the babies, find food, and keep watch for danger. The young ones play together, chase each other through the trees, and learn how to be good members of the group. Monkeys groom each other, which means they gently pick through each other's fur. This is not just about staying clean. It is also a way of saying, I care about you, and we are friends. When a monkey is sad or scared, another monkey will often sit close and comfort it.

## How monkeys talk to each other

Monkeys do not speak words like we do, but they have plenty to say. They use sounds, faces and body movements to talk to each other. A loud call can warn the troop that danger is near. A soft sound can mean, come here, I found food. A monkey can show it is happy by playing, or show it is cross by making a stern face. Some monkeys have very loud voices that can be heard far away through the forest. Others use quiet chirps and squeaks. A monkey's face is full of expression too. It can look surprised, sleepy, playful or worried, just like a human face. People who spend time with monkeys learn to read these signs, and that helps them understand how the animals are feeling.

## Monkeys and people, long ago and today

People and monkeys have lived near each other for thousands of years. In some places, monkeys live in towns and villages, and people see them every day. In other places, people visit forests and parks specially to watch them. Monkeys have helped scientists learn a great deal about how brains work, how families care for each other, and how animals learn. Because monkeys are so much like us, studying them helps doctors and scientists understand people better too. In many stories and tales from around the world, monkeys are the clever heroes who outsmart bigger animals with quick thinking. That is because people have always admired how smart and quick monkeys are.

## Helping monkeys stay safe

Today, many monkeys need our help. Their forest homes are being cut down to make room for farms, roads and towns. When the forest disappears, monkeys lose their food, their shelter and their safe places to live. Some monkeys are also taken from the wild to be kept as pets, but a monkey is not a good pet. Monkeys need space, friends, fresh food and trees to climb, and they can be unhappy and unhealthy in a house. The best way to help monkeys is to protect their forests and to visit them in zoos and wildlife parks that care for them well. When we protect forests, we also protect clean air, fresh water and many other animals that share the forest with monkeys.

## What we can learn from monkeys

Monkeys teach us wonderful lessons. They show us that family matters, that friends help each other, and that learning never stops. They remind us to be curious, to try new things, and to keep trying when a problem is hard. A monkey that cannot crack a nut the first time will try again and again until it works out how. They also teach us to play. Play is not just fun for monkeys. It is how young monkeys learn to climb, to share, and to get along with others. When we watch monkeys play, we can see that play is an important part of growing up for animals and for people too. If we care for monkeys and their homes, these clever cousins will be swinging through the trees for many years to come.

## Fun facts about monkeys

- Some monkeys use their tails like an extra hand to hold on to branches while they reach for food.
- A baby monkey holds on tight to its mother's fur and rides on her back or belly as she moves through the trees.
- Monkeys groom each other to stay clean and to show friendship and care.
- Some monkeys wash their food in water before eating it.
- Monkeys can learn new skills by watching other monkeys and copying what they do.
