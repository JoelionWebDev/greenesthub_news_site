Imagine walking down a busy street with your eyes closed. How would you know when to stop, when to turn, or when a step is in front of you? For people who are blind, or who cannot see very well, the answer is often a very special friend with four legs and a clever mind. That friend is a guide dog, a trained helper that leads its owner safely through the world and gives people freedom, safety, and friendship all at the same time.

## What is a guide dog?

A guide dog is a dog that has been specially trained to help a person who is blind or has very poor sight. The dog wears a special harness, which is like a handle that the person holds. Through the harness, the person can feel which way the dog is moving. The dog walks in front, just a little bit ahead, and guides the person around anything in the way.

Guide dogs are trained to stop at every kerb, the edge of the pavement, and wait for the person to tell them it is safe to cross the road. They do not decide when to cross on their own. The person listens for traffic and decides, and the dog follows the instruction. Most guide dogs are Labradors, Golden Retrievers, or a mix of the two, because they are gentle, clever, and calm in busy places.

A guide dog is a working dog, not a pet to stroke in the street. When it is wearing its harness, it needs to focus, so never distract it by calling it or offering it food.

## How do guide dogs learn?

Guide dogs do not learn their job in a day. Their training takes a long time, and it starts when they are still little puppies. When a guide dog puppy is just a few weeks old, it goes to live with a special family called a puppy walker. This kind volunteer looks after the puppy for about a year, teaching it to come when called, to walk nicely on a lead, and to be calm in busy places. The puppy walker takes the puppy to shops, on buses, and to parks, so it grows up feeling happy and relaxed everywhere.

When the puppy is about a year old, it goes to a training school for guide dogs. A professional trainer teaches it the special skills of the job: walking in a straight line, stopping at kerbs, walking around things in the way, and finding doors, crossings, and seats. It learns to stop if something is hanging low, like a branch, that could bump its owner's head, and to ignore food on the ground, because a guide dog must always focus on its owner.

Not every dog becomes a guide dog. Some dogs are too playful, too shy, or too easily distracted. Those dogs are not failures. They go on to live happy lives as family pets, or they may be trained for another kind of helpful work. Only the calmest and most focused dogs graduate, and each one is matched with a person who needs it.

## A day in the life of a guide dog

A guide dog's day starts early, just like its owner's day. In the morning, the dog may help its owner walk to the bus stop or the train station, guiding them through the station and finding a seat. Then it might guide them to work, to school, or to the shops. Guide dogs go almost everywhere their owners go, including shops, restaurants, buses, trains, and offices, because the law in many countries says a guide dog must be allowed to go with its owner.

During the day, the dog works hard, but it also has time to rest. When the owner sits down, the dog lies down quietly and waits, and it can spring into action as soon as its owner stands up. At home in the evening, the harness comes off, and the guide dog becomes a family pet. It can run around, play with toys, and cuddle up with its owner.

## The bond between people and guide dogs

People have been keeping dogs as helpers for thousands of years. Dogs were some of the first animals to live with people, and they have guarded our homes, helped us hunt, and kept us company ever since. Guide dogs are a modern part of that long friendship. The first guide dog schools were started about a hundred years ago, to help soldiers who had lost their sight in war. Since then, guide dog schools have grown all over the world.

There is something very special about the trust between a guide dog and its owner. The owner cannot see the dangers around them, so they put their safety in the paws of their dog. The dog, in turn, works hard every day to keep its owner safe. It is a partnership built on love, trust, and teamwork.

## How to be kind to a guide dog

If you ever meet a guide dog, there are some simple ways to be kind. Remember that a working guide dog is not a pet to play with, so do not call it, whistle at it, or try to feed it. Do not touch the dog or its harness without asking the owner first. If a person with a guide dog looks lost, you can ask politely if they would like some help, but do not take the dog's harness or pull the dog. You can also help by keeping pavements clear, so a guide dog does not have to walk around things in the way. Being kind to guide dogs is a way of being kind to the people they help.
