Have you ever watched someone run really fast and wished you could run that fast too? Now imagine running so fast that the whole world blurs past you. That is what life is like for the cheetah, the fastest land animal on Earth. Cheetahs live on the grasslands of Africa, and they can run at speeds of around 60 to 70 kilometres per hour — faster than most cars driving through a town! But the cheetah is much more than just a fast runner. It is a beautiful, graceful, and clever hunter with some very special features.

## A Body Built for Speed

Look closely at a cheetah and you will see that its whole body is made for running fast. It has long, slim legs, a small head, and a light, narrow body. Unlike other big cats, a cheetah's body is not heavy and muscular. It is slim and light, like a racing athlete.

A cheetah's spine is very flexible — it bends and stretches like a spring. When the cheetah runs, its spine stretches out to make its stride longer, and then curls up to bring its back legs forward again. Each stride can cover a huge distance. A cheetah's long tail works like a rudder on a boat, helping it balance and steer when it turns sharply at high speed.

Another special feature is the cheetah's big chest and large lungs. When a cheetah runs, it needs a lot of air. Its large lungs and strong heart pump oxygen around its body very quickly. But even so, a cheetah cannot run at top speed for long. A super-fast chase usually lasts less than a minute, because the cheetah's body gets very hot and tired. If the cheetah does not catch its prey quickly, it has to stop and rest.

## Spots and Tear Lines

Every cheetah has a beautiful coat covered in solid black spots. No two cheetahs have exactly the same pattern of spots — just like no two people have exactly the same fingerprints! These spots help the cheetah hide in the tall golden grass of the savanna. When a cheetah crouches down low, its spotted coat breaks up its shape, so the animals it hunts may not notice it until it is too late.

Look at a cheetah's face and you will see two dark lines running from the corners of its eyes down to its mouth. These are called "tear lines." Scientists think these dark lines work a bit like the black paint that sports players wear under their eyes — they help cut down the glare of the bright African sun, so the cheetah can see clearly when it is hunting in daylight.

## How a Cheetah Hunts

Cheetahs hunt during the day, which is different from most other big cats. Lions and leopards often hunt at night, but cheetahs prefer the daylight because they hunt with their eyes.

First, the cheetah finds a high spot, like a termite mound or a low tree branch, and looks around for prey. It watches animals like small antelopes and hares. When it picks its target, it creeps closer and closer, keeping its body low in the grass. It moves slowly and quietly, step by step, until it is close enough to start the chase.

Then — whoosh! The cheetah bursts into action. In just a few seconds, it reaches its top speed. It chases the prey across the grassland, twisting and turning to follow every move. The prey animal runs and dodges, but the cheetah's speed and agility often win. At the end of the chase, the cheetah uses its paw to trip the prey, then catches it.

After a chase, the cheetah is exhausted. It needs to rest and catch its breath before it can eat. This is a dangerous time for a cheetah, because other animals, like lions and hyenas, may try to steal its food. A tired cheetah often has to give up its meal and walk away. That is why cheetahs hunt often — they do not always get to keep what they catch.

## Cheetah Families

Female cheetahs live alone most of the time, except when they are raising cubs. A mother cheetah usually has several cubs at a time. When the cubs are born, they are tiny and helpless, and they have a fluffy grey mane of fur along their backs. This mane makes them look a little like a honey badger, a fierce little animal that most predators leave alone. Scientists think this disguise might help protect the cubs from danger.

Mother cheetahs are very caring and brave. They move their cubs to new hiding places every few days to keep them safe from lions, hyenas and other dangers. The mother brings back food for her cubs and teaches them how to hunt. Cubs play a lot — they chase each other, pounce on leaves, and practise stalking. All this play is really practice for the real hunts they will do when they grow up.

Cubs stay with their mother for over a year. When they are old enough, the female cubs go off to live alone, while the male cubs often stay together in small groups called coalitions. Brothers that stay together can help each other hunt and protect a territory. Teamwork helps them survive in a world full of bigger, stronger animals.

## Where Do Cheetahs Live?

Cheetahs live mostly in Africa, on the wide open grasslands called savannas. They need open spaces to run, so they do best where the grass is tall and the land is flat. There are also a small number of cheetahs living in Iran, in Asia. Long ago, cheetahs lived in many more places, including parts of Asia and even southern Europe, but today their homes have shrunk.

Cheetahs do not live in forests, because trees get in the way of their high-speed chases. They also avoid places with too many lions and hyenas, because those animals steal their food and can harm their cubs. This means cheetahs need large areas of safe, open grassland to live in.

Cheetahs are special animals. They are not just the fastest runners on land — they are also gentle, graceful, and beautiful. They remind us that speed is not the only thing that matters. A cheetah also needs sharp eyes, clever hunting skills, and a brave mother to teach it. The next time you run fast on the playground, remember the cheetah — the animal that runs faster than any other on land, with spots, tear lines, and a tail that steers it like a rudder.
