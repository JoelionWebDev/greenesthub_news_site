High in the sky, a falcon folds its wings and dives towards the ground faster than almost any other animal on Earth. Far below, a person watches, raises a gloved hand, and waits. The falcon swoops back up and lands gracefully on the person's glove. This is falconry — the ancient art of hunting with birds of prey — and people have been doing it for thousands of years. Kings and queens, knights and nobles all loved falconry. Let us discover this amazing friendship between humans and falcons.

## What Is Falconry?

Falconry is the art of training a bird of prey — like a falcon, hawk or eagle — to hunt with a person. The falconer (the person who trains the bird) and the falcon work together as a team. The falcon flies free in the sky, hunts its prey, and then returns to the falconer for a reward of food.

Falconry is not like keeping a pet. A falcon is a wild animal, and it is never truly tame. The falcon stays with the falconer because it trusts the falconer and knows that the falconer will give it food. The bond between a falconer and a falcon is built on trust, patience, and respect.

The most commonly used bird in falconry is the peregrine falcon. Peregrines are famous for being the fastest animals in the world when they dive. When a peregrine spots prey from high in the sky, it folds its wings and dives — called a "stoop" — at an incredible speed.

## A History as Old as Kings

Falconry is one of the oldest sports in the world. People began practising it thousands of years ago, probably in Central Asia, where nomadic people first learned to train birds of prey. From there, falconry spread to China, the Middle East, and Europe.

In medieval Europe, falconry was the sport of kings and queens. Kings would ride out with their falcons on their gloves, followed by their courtiers and hunting dogs. Different ranks of people were supposed to fly different birds — kings flew gyrfalcons, princes flew peregrines, and knights flew sakers. Falconry was not just a sport; it was a sign of wealth, power, and nobility.

In the Middle East, falconry has been loved for centuries too. Arab falconers hunted in the desert with their falcons, and falconry is still a proud tradition there today. In Japan, falconry was practised by samurai warriors. All over the world, people discovered the joy of flying a bird of prey.

## How a Falconer Trains a Falcon

Training a falcon takes a lot of time, patience, and care. First, the falconer gets the falcon used to sitting on the glove and being around people. The falconer talks softly to the bird and offers it small pieces of food, so the falcon learns that the falconer is a friend, not a danger.

Next, the falconer teaches the falcon to fly to the glove for food. At first, the falcon only flies a short distance. Gradually, the distance gets longer. The falconer uses a special whistle or call, and the falcon learns that the sound means food is waiting.

Falconers use special equipment. The falcon wears small leather straps called jesses on its legs, which help the falconer hold the bird safely. The falcon also wears a soft leather hood that covers its eyes and keeps it calm when it is not flying. The falconer wears a thick leather glove to protect the hand from the falcon's sharp talons. There is also a lure — a piece of meat attached to a cord that the falconer swings around to call the falcon back.

Training a falcon is never rushed. A good falconer is patient and gentle, because a frightened or stressed falcon will not learn. The falconer must understand the bird's moods and needs, and always treat it with respect.

## A Day of Falconry

On a hunting day, the falconer carries the falcon on the glove to the hunting grounds. The falconer releases the bird, and the falcon flies up high, circling in the sky. It uses its amazing eyesight — far better than a human's — to look for prey far below.

When the falconer flushes out prey, like a small bird, the falcon dives. If the hunt is successful, the falcon catches the prey. The falconer then offers the falcon a reward of food, and takes the prey. Then the falcon is ready to fly again.

Falconry is not just about hunting. Many falconers today fly their birds for the joy of it, without hunting. They enjoy watching the falcon soar, dive, and return to the glove. Falconry is a way to spend time outdoors, in nature, with a magnificent wild bird.

## Falcons in the Wild

Falcons are amazing birds even without falconers. The peregrine falcon lives on every continent except Antarctica. It can live in cities as well as in wild places, and it often nests on tall buildings, which are a bit like the cliffs it nests on in nature. Peregrines eat other birds, catching them in mid-air with incredible skill.

Falcons have special features that make them great hunters. They have sharp, curved beaks for tearing food, powerful talons for catching prey, and excellent eyesight. A falcon can spot a small bird from very far away. Falcons also have a special notch in their beak, called a "tomial tooth," which helps them catch and kill their prey quickly.

## Falconry Today

Falconry is still practised all over the world today. In many countries, falconers must have a licence, and they must follow strict rules to protect wild birds. Falconers help with conservation too. In the past, peregrine falcons became very rare because of harmful chemicals used in farming. Falconers helped to breed peregrines and release them back into the wild, which helped the species recover. Today, peregrines are doing much better, thanks in part to the work of falconers.

Falconry is recognised by UNESCO — the United Nations organisation for culture — as an important part of human heritage. It is one of the few traditions that has been passed down through generations for thousands of years, almost unchanged.

Falconry teaches us something wonderful: that humans and wild animals can work together, not by forcing the animal, but by building trust. The falcon is always free — it could fly away at any moment and never come back. But it chooses to return to the falconer's glove, again and again. That is a friendship as old as kings — and it is still alive today.
